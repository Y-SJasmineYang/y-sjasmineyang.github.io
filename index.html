About Me

Hi, I am Yung-Shiang Jasmine Yang. I am a Research Associate (Postdoctoral Researcher) at the University of Galway and a PhD Candidate in Economics at University College Dublin (UCD).

My viva is scheduled for January 2027, and I am on the 2026–2027 academic job market.

My research is in behavioural economics. I use experiments and theoretical models to study how people make decisions, both individually and collectively.

One part of my work looks at individual choices: for example, how motivation, algorithmic discrimination, ambiguity aversion, or rank information affects decisions. I am also interested in cooperation and collective decision-making.

At Galway, I work on the LE CHÉILE (Local Examples of Cooperation and Harnessing Experimental Insights to Lower Emissions) project.

Job Market Paper

Discriminated but not Discouraged: Investment Responses to Algorithmic Discrimination

Yung-Shiang Jasmine Yang (2026) (Submitted)

Abstract Hide Abstract

We study how individuals adapt their investment when an algorithm evaluates groups differently. In a pre-registered online experiment (N = 553), participants are randomly assigned to an advantaged or disadvantaged identity group and choose how much to invest to improve their probability of being hired by an algorithm that holds different prior beliefs across groups. Disadvantaged participants invest 17.7% more than advantaged participants, showing no evidence of discouragement in investment despite facing less favorable evaluation. We then test two interventions: “Role Model” treatments providing information about the behavior and outcomes of same-group predecessors, and a “No Risk” treatment that conditions payment of the investment cost on being hired. The “Role Model” treatments have limited effects, whereas “No Risk” increases investment among disadvantaged participants by 24%. This effect is significantly larger than the corresponding effect among advantaged participants. Our findings show that individuals can respond to algorithmic disadvantage by increasing rather than reducing investment, and that changing the economic consequences of investment generates a stronger behavioral response than same-group social information.

Motivation in All-Pay Contests

Yung-Shiang Jasmine Yang (2026) Journal of Public Economic Theory, 28(1), e70100.

Abstract Hide Abstract

This paper studies how agents with monetary and non-monetary motivation interact strategically in competitions. Examining the question under an all-pay contest framework, we show that there do not exist pure-strategy equilibria when the competing agents share the same motivation. When agents with different motivation compete against each other, we determine the conditions for the existence of pure-strategy equilibria and fully characterize the mixed-strategy equilibria. The results help explain situations where prizes are ineffective in eliciting higher efforts in contests. They show that the discouragement effect is prevalent when competing agents harbor different motivation and illustrate how reputation and money can be complementary goods.

Working Papers & Projects

Does Ambiguity Aversion Stem from Misperceptions of Variance? Some Experimental Evidence

Yung-Shiang Jasmine Yang, Margaret Samahita, Karl Whelan (In progress)

Abstract Hide Abstract

While ambiguity aversion is well documented, it is unclear what motivates it. One possibility is that people believe more ambiguous payoffs imply greater uncertainty, in the form of higher variance. In an online experiment, we construct two lotteries that differ only in ambiguity while holding expected payoffs and payoff variance constant, and test whether informing participants that the lotteries have identical expected payoffs and variance affects ambiguity aversion. Participants first choose between the two lotteries and then reveal their ambiguity premium through an incentive-compatible switching task. Although the information treatment improves participants' understanding of the lotteries' statistical properties, it does not significantly reduce the ambiguity premium, nor is the treatment effect different among participants with correct ex-ante understanding. Text analyses of participants' stated motivation show that the information treatment changes how participants explain their decisions, despite their ambiguity premium being unaffected. Our findings suggest that correcting misconceptions about payoff variance alone is insufficient to substantially reduce ambiguity aversion.
