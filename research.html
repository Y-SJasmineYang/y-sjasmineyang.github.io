Research

Publications

  • Yang, Y. S. J. (2026). Motivation in All-Pay Contests. Journal of Public Economic Theory, 28(1), e70100.

    This paper studies how agents with monetary and non-monetary motivation interact strategically in competitions. Examining the question under an all-pay contest framework, we show that there do not exist pure-strategy equilibria when the competing agents share the same motivation. When agents with different motivation compete against each other, we determine the conditions for the existence of pure-strategy equilibria and fully characterize the mixed-strategy equilibria. The results help explain situations where prizes are ineffective in eliciting higher efforts in contests. They show that the discouragement effect is prevalent when competing agents harbor different motivation and illustrate how reputation and money can be complementary goods.

Working Papers

  • Discriminated but not Discouraged: Investment Responses to Algorithmic Discrimination (Job Market Paper; submitted)

    We study how individuals adapt their investment when an algorithm evaluates groups differently. In a pre-registered online experiment (N = 553), participants are randomly assigned to an advantaged or disadvantaged identity group and choose how much to invest to improve their probability of being hired by an algorithm that holds different prior beliefs across groups. Disadvantaged participants invest 17.7% more than advantaged participants, showing no evidence of discouragement in investment despite facing less favorable evaluation. We then test two interventions: “Role Model” treatments providing information about the behavior and outcomes of same-group predecessors, and a “No Risk” treatment that conditions payment of the investment cost on being hired. The “Role Model” treatments have limited effects, whereas “No Risk” increases investment among disadvantaged participants by 24%. This effect is significantly larger than the corresponding effect among advantaged participants. Our findings show that individuals can respond to algorithmic disadvantage by increasing rather than reducing investment, and that changing the economic consequences of investment generates a stronger behavioral response than same-group social information.
